01

Introduction — What Are Financial Statements?

Every company needs to record all its transactions — how much it sold, bought, owes, and owns. For this, companies maintain Books of Accounts at their registered/located office. From these books, the company prepares its Final Accounts / Financial Statements at year-end.

Books of Accounts — What Companies Maintain
  • Cash Book — every cash transaction
  • Purchase Book — all purchases
  • Sales Book — all sales
  • Bills & Vouchers — supporting documents for each transaction
  • Documents & Registers — official company records
  • Minutes — records of board/company meetings
📌 Format of Records
Both paper and electronic form are allowed. Companies can maintain their books digitally — both are equally valid.
What Goes Into the Books?

Every income, every expense, every sale, every purchase, every asset the company owns, every liability it has — all of this gets recorded. The books are the raw data from which financial statements are built.

Cost Accounting — A Special Case

Manufacturing and Mining companies additionally do Cost Accounting — separately tracking costs for material, labour, and overheads. This is a separate subject (Cost Accounting) you'll study in a later semester. In this chapter, we only deal with financial accounting.

03

Key Basics — Accrual, Double Entry & Financial Year

Accrual Basis

Record ALL transactions of the current year — whether cash or credit. The only condition: the transaction must belong to this accounting year.

Cash sale of ₹10K → record it. Credit sale of ₹5K → also record it. Both belong to this year.

Double Entry System

Every transaction has a dual effect — debit one account, credit another. This is why the Balance Sheet always balances.

All financial statements are built on this system. Assets = Liabilities + Equity always holds.

Financial Year
📅 Default Financial Year
1st April → 31st March (12 months). This is the default for all Indian companies.

Exception: If a company's transactions naturally end on 31st December (like MNCs with global reporting requirements), they can have a Jan–Dec financial year. But unless stated, always assume April 1 to March 31.

05

Components of Financial Statements (Schedule III)

Companies prepare four financial statements under Schedule III. Each serves a different purpose:

1. Balance Sheet

Shows the company's financial position on a specific date. What it owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and owner's stake (equity). A snapshot of the company.

2. Statement of Profit & Loss

Also called Income Statement. Shows all income & gains AND all expenses & losses for the year. Tells you: is the company profitable or not?

3. Cash Flow Statement

Records all cash inflows (money coming in) and cash outflows (money going out) during the year. Covered in a separate chapter later.

4. Notes to Accounts

Detailed working notes and breakup for every item shown in the Balance Sheet. BS shows the totals; Notes show what's inside each total.

💡 Notes to Accounts — How it Works
Your Balance Sheet shows "Non-Current Assets: ₹5,00,000." Want to know what those assets are exactly? Come to Notes to Accounts. It gives the full breakup. The Balance Sheet is the summary; Notes are the detail. Both run side by side throughout this chapter.

I. Shareholders' Fund — Sub-head (a): Share Capital

Both equity shares AND preference shares are shown here. The Share Capital note works in a sequence — each step builds on the previous:

TypeMeaningExample from Video
Authorised CapitalThe MAXIMUM limit the company is permitted/authorised to issue. Fixed in Memorandum of Association. You cannot exceed this."I am authorised to issue 2000 equity shares @ ₹100. My max = ₹2,00,000."
Issued CapitalHow much the company ACTUALLY issued to the public (must be ≤ Authorised)"Out of 2000 allowed, I actually issued 1880 shares."
Subscribed CapitalHow much the public actually APPLIED FOR and got allotment (may be ≤ Issued)"Public applied for 1800 shares and got them."
Called-up CapitalHow much of the subscribed amount the company has DEMANDED from shareholders so far (called in installments)"Share is ₹10. I've called ₹7 so far. ₹3 will be called later."
Paid-up CapitalCalled-up capital MINUS Call in Arrears. What shareholders have ACTUALLY PAID."Called ₹7 per share. Some paid only ₹6. Arrears = ₹1. Paid-up = Called-up − ₹1."
📐 Key Formula
Paid-up Capital = Called-up Capital − Call in Arrears
💡 Why Not Collect Full Price at Once?
Companies collect share price in installments — application money, allotment money, first call, second call, etc. This is easier for investors. "Call in Arrears" = the called portion that a shareholder has not yet paid. It gets subtracted from called-up to get paid-up.
I. Shareholders' Fund — Sub-head (b): Reserves & Surplus

All types of reserves come here. The Balance of Statement of P&L (net profit carried forward) also sits here:

  • General Reserve
  • WCR (Working Capital Reserve)
  • IFR (Investment Fluctuation Reserve)
  • Contingency Reserve
  • Capital Redemption Reserve
  • Securities Premium Reserve ← important, see uses below
  • Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)
  • Revaluation Reserve
  • Share Option Outstanding Account
  • Balance of Statement of P&L — the net profit (or loss) carried forward lives here
⚠️ Securities Premium Reserve — Permitted Uses (Exam Favourite!)
Securities Premium is restricted — you can ONLY use it for these purposes:
  1. Issue of Bonus Shares
  2. Writing off Preliminary Expenses
  3. Writing off Underwriting Commissions
  4. Payment of Redemption Premium on shares/debentures (premium to premium rule)
  5. Creating Debenture Redemption Reserve
  6. Creating Revaluation Reserve
  7. Share Option Outstanding Account
This is tested directly — "For what purposes can Securities Premium Reserve be used?"
I. Shareholders' Fund — Sub-head (c): Money Received Against Share Warrants
📖 Definition
A Share Warrant gives a shareholder the right to purchase shares at today's price at a future date. The company collects a small down payment now for locking in this right. That down payment = Money Received Against Share Warrants.
💡 Analogy (from the video)
Think of booking a flat or plot. You pay a booking amount today → the builder locks the property for you at today's price → later you pay the full amount and take possession. Similarly: Shareholder pays a small amount now → company locks those shares at today's price → shareholder returns later to purchase at the agreed price. That initial booking amount sitting with the company = Money Received Against Share Warrants.
II. Share Application Money Pending Allotment

Application money has been received BUT shares have not yet been allotted.

⚠️ Critical Rule
Non-refundable portion → Share Application Money Pending Allotment
Refundable portion → Other Current Liabilities

Logic: If we're going to return the money, it's our current liability. If we're keeping it (they didn't get shares but we won't refund), it sits in this separate head.
III. Non-Current Liabilities (Long-term: beyond 12 months)
HeadWhat Goes Here
Long-term BorrowingsLoans, debentures, bonds that won't be repaid within 12 months / operating cycle
Deferred Tax Liability (DTL)Tax owed to the government in the future — detailed in Section 10
Other Long-term LiabilitiesTrade payables for purchase of Fixed Assets (bought machinery on credit → long-term), Provident Fund contributions, advance received for long-term contracts
Long-term ProvisionsProvisions for long-term obligations — e.g., long-term warranty provisions, gratuity provisions
IV. Current Liabilities (Short-term: within 12 months / operating cycle)
HeadWhat Goes Here
Short-term BorrowingsLoans and advances to be repaid within 12 months — cash credit, bank overdraft, short-term loans

🧠 Memory Trick (Directly from the Video!)
Non-Current and Current Liabilities are mirror images of each other:

Long-term Borrowings ↔ Short-term Borrowings
Other LT Liabilities ↔ Other Current Liabilities
Long-term Provisions ↔ Short-term Provisions
(Only change: DTL in non-current, Trade Payables in current)

Memorise the Non-Current heads → Current heads follow automatically!

(d) Long-term Loans & Advances — Two types:

Capital Advances

Advance given for the purchase of a Fixed Asset (PPE).

Repayment comes as an ASSET, not cash.

Other LT Loans & Advances

Long-term but repaid in CASH (not as assets).

These include long-term deposits given, staff advances, etc.

(e) Other Non-Current Assets — Long-term receivables, security deposits, unamortized losses/expenses — anything long-term that doesn't fit the above categories.

Current vs Non-Current — Operating Cycle Explained

📖 Operating Cycle (Definition)
The time period between acquiring an asset → processing/using it → converting back into Cash or Cash Equivalents.

This is the natural business rhythm. How long does one full business cycle take?
💡 Example
A bakery: Buy flour (acquire asset) → Make bread (process) → Sell bread and collect cash (realize) = one complete operating cycle. Could be a few days, a few months, or over a year depending on the nature of the business.
TypeTimeframeIf Not Mentioned in Question
CurrentSettled/realized within operating cycle OR 12 months — whichever comes firstAssume 12 months as the operating cycle default
Non-CurrentEverything that is NOT current — beyond the operating cycle / 12 months
⚠️ Exam Strategy (from the video)
Just identify what is CURRENT. Everything else automatically becomes NON-CURRENT.

If the question gives an operating cycle (e.g., "operating cycle is 8 months") → use that as your cutoff.
If NOT given → assume 12 months. This is a key decision point in classification questions.

Liability vs Provision vs Reserve — The Differences

This is a commonly confused topic. All three involve setting aside money for future needs, but they differ in one key way:

FeatureLiabilityProvisionReserve
Know you have to pay?✅ Yes✅ Yes⚠️ General safety
Know the EXACT amount?✅ Yes (certain)❌ No (estimated)❌ No
Know the reason/cause?✅ Yes — specific✅ Yes — specific cause, unknown amount❌ No specific reason — general
SourceRegular business obligationFrom profit (charge against profit)From profit (appropriation of profit)
ExampleSalary payable ₹20,000 — exact amount, exact obligationProvision for bad debts — know some debtors will default, not how much exactlyGeneral Reserve — saving profits "just in case" for the future
📖 Liability (short)
You KNOW you have to pay AND you KNOW EXACTLY HOW MUCH. Both cause and amount are certain.
📖 Provision (short)
You KNOW WHAT it's for (specific cause). But you DON'T KNOW HOW MUCH. Cause certain, amount uncertain → estimate it.
📌 Reserve vs Provision
Reserve: Don't even know exactly what you're saving for. Just setting aside profits for general future safety or unexpected losses. Neither cause nor amount is certain.

Provision: You KNOW the specific reason/cause. You just DON'T KNOW the exact amount. You estimate it.

Both come from profits. The distinction is the specificity of the cause.

Deferred Tax — DTA & DTL (Full Concept + Examples)

This is one of the trickier concepts in this chapter. Take it step by step — it's not as hard as it looks.

📖 The Core Idea — Two Types of Income
Accounting Income — Income recorded in your Books of Accounts (as per accounting rules like GAAP/Ind AS)
Taxable Income — Income calculated as per Income Tax Law (tax rules, Income Tax Act)

These two can be different from each other. The difference between them = Deferred Tax.

"Deferred" means postponed/pushed to later. The tax effect is being deferred to the future.
Case 1: Deferred Tax Liability (DTL)

Condition: Accounting Income > Taxable Income

What happened: Earned more but showed less to tax → paid LESS tax than you should have

Result: You OWE the government more tax in future → LIABILITY

Accounting Income = ₹20L → Tax@40% = ₹8L
Taxable Income   = ₹15L → Tax@40% = ₹6L
─────────────────────────────────────
DTL = ₹8L − ₹6L = ₹2 lakh
(Under-paid by ₹2L → owe govt)

Journal Entry:
Dr. Profit & Loss A/c   ₹2L
Cr. Deferred Tax Liability ₹2L

Case 2: Deferred Tax Asset (DTA)

Condition: Taxable Income > Accounting Income

What happened: Showed more income to tax authorities → paid MORE tax than needed

Result: Government OWES you a refund in future → ASSET

Accounting Income = ₹15L → Tax@40% = ₹6L
Taxable Income   = ₹20L → Tax@40% = ₹8L
─────────────────────────────────────
DTA = ₹8L − ₹6L = ₹2 lakh
(Over-paid by ₹2L → govt owes you)
📐 Simple Memory Rule
Give extra tax to government in future → DTL (Non-Current Liability)
Government gives extra tax back to you → DTA (Non-Current Asset)
📌 Where They Appear in Balance Sheet
DTL → Under Non-Current Liabilities (Equity & Liabilities side)
DTA → Under Non-Current Assets (Assets side)

Detailed Breakdown of Each Line Item
Line ItemDetailed Explanation
Revenue from OperationsSales revenue (main business activity). Revenue from services rendered. Other operating revenue directly tied to daily operations.
Other IncomeDividend received on investments, Profit on sale of investments or assets, Rent received, Interest earned on deposits, foreign exchange gains — anything NOT from main day-to-day operations.
Changes in InventoriesFormula: Opening Stock − Closing Stock. If opening > closing, you consumed/sold more inventory = cost increases. If closing > opening = you accumulated stock = cost reduces. Covers WIP, Finished Goods, Stock-in-Trade.

Extraordinary Items"Acts of God" — completely unforeseeable, rare events: flood damage, earthquake loss, fire loss, tsunami. You could NOT have predicted these. They are deducted separately for clarity.
Earnings Per Share (EPS)
📐 Basic EPS Formula
Basic EPS = Net Profit After Tax ÷ Weighted Average Number of Shares

Answers: "For every share, how much did the company earn this year?"
Higher EPS = more earning per share = company doing well = more attractive to investors.
📖 Basic EPS
Calculated for a specific financial period WITHOUT considering any special obligations like convertible instruments. It's the plain, straightforward EPS — no complex adjustments. This is what you'll mostly work with in this course.

📖 What is CSR?
CSR makes companies socially responsible. They can't just operate for their own profit — they must contribute to society's welfare too. As Purnima put it: "कब तक अपना ही अपना करोगे? थोड़ा सा सोसाइटी के लिए भी करना बनता है।"

📌 Meeting ANY ONE is enough
You don't need to meet all three. If your net profit crosses ₹5 Cr even if your turnover is only ₹200 Cr — CSR still applies.
How Much Must Be Spent?
📐 CSR Spending Formula
2% of Average Net Profit of Last 3 Years

Step 1: Add up net profits of the last 3 years → divide by 3 = average net profit

Key Terms — Divisible Profit, Surplus, Dividend & EPS

Divisible Profit
📖 Definition
After deducting all expenses, losses, depreciation from total profit AND after setting aside required reserves — the remaining amount that can actually be distributed to shareholders = Divisible Profit.

Simply: "The profit you're legally and actually allowed to give out to shareholders."
Surplus
📖 Definition
The balance remaining after ALL adjustments in the Reserves & Surplus account for the year. The company then decides: how much to keep in reserves (retain) and how much to distribute as dividend.

Surplus tells you: "What's left after everything is accounted for — now what do we do with it?"
Reserve & Surplus — How to Calculate
Opening Balance of Reserves & Surplus + Net Profit for the year ± Prior Period Adjustments / Corrections ← errors from past years corrected − Transfer to General Reserve ← company's choice (no fixed % now) − Interim Dividend already paid ───────────────────────────────────────── = Closing Balance of Reserves & Surplus ← shown in Balance Sheet
⚠️ Rule Change — Transfer to Reserve
Old rule: Companies HAD to transfer a fixed percentage of profits to reserves mandatorily.
New rule (current): No fixed rule. Company can CHOOSE whether to transfer to reserves or not. Completely optional now. This is a common exam question!
Dividend Tax — Important Rule Change
⚠️ Major Rule Change (Exam Favourite!)
Old rule: Company paid Dividend Distribution Tax (DDT) when it declared dividends.
New rule (current): SHAREHOLDERS pay the dividend tax on dividends they receive — not the company.

This is frequently tested. Know both the old and new rule!
Types of Dividend
Interim Dividend

Paid BEFORE year-end annual profit is officially declared.

Mid-year distribution — like an advance on expected dividends. Based on estimated current-year profits.

Declared by the Board of Directors alone (no shareholder approval needed).

In accounts: Deducted from Surplus — treated as paid in current year itself.

Proposed Dividend

Declared AT YEAR-END from current year's final profits.

Proposed by Board of Directors → must be approved at the Annual General Meeting (AGM).

As per revised AS rules: treated as a contingent liability — goes to next year's accounts after AGM approval.

Earnings Per Share (EPS) — Full Picture
📐 Basic EPS Formula
Basic EPS = Net Profit After Tax ÷ Weighted Average Number of Shares

Answers: "For every one share of this company, how much profit was earned this year?"

Investors use EPS to compare companies and gauge earning potential. Higher EPS = better return per share = stronger investment case.
📌 Why EPS Matters (from the video)
EPS is one of the most important numbers for investors. Before investing, they check EPS to understand earning potential. If EPS is good, they invest. If EPS is growing year on year, even better. Your financial statements reveal this EPS figure.
